Every dot is one crash report filed with NHTSA under the Standing General Order for an automated driving system, colored by the weather box the operator ticked. Fog, snow and wind together are six reports, so rain is the only adverse condition this analysis can test.

On its own, 3.9 percent says nothing about risk. It would be a lot in Seattle and a lot of nothing in Phoenix. The whole story is finding the right denominator.

Tasks and audience

Team task: let a curious non expert explore when, where and how ADS vehicles crash. My task: weather, so a reader can compare crash rates across conditions and judge whether rain raises the risk.

Audience: regulators, journalists and riders who have heard that sensors fail in rain and want evidence. They know percentages, not intervals, so every number gets a plain sentence.

2 of 5 · The denominator

It rained far more often than the reports say it did

For each of the six metros, take its crash count and multiply by the share of hours it was actually raining there, from an hourly weather archive over the same months. That is how many rain crashes there would be if rain made no difference. Each ring is one of those predicted crashes. Each filled dot is a rain crash that was really reported.

187 predicted. 91 reported. Every metro has empty rings, and the rainiest cities, Austin and Atlanta, have the most. This is the step where the hypothesis flipped.

The reports contain no miles, so the slider asks the obvious question: how much less would the fleets have to drive in rain for the rings to fill? The answer is about half.

3 of 5 · Chance

No rain neutral world comes anywhere near 91

Could 91 against 187 be luck? Rerun the world a thousand times. Each world keeps every metro's real crash count and real rain hours, but lets each crash land in rain purely by chance, with probability equal to the share of hours it rained there. The bars count the rain crashes each world produced; the blue line is what really happened.

The worlds land between roughly 150 and 230. None of them gets close to 91. Pick a single metro to see how much it can say alone: San Francisco is decisive by itself; Las Vegas, with one rain crash, sits inside its own cloud and tells us nothing either way.

4 of 5 · Place and time

The fleets spread city by city, and rain crashes follow each city's rainy season

Every bubble is a city with at least one report; area is the number of reports. Press play and the map fills in the order the fleets expanded: San Francisco first, then Phoenix, Los Angeles, Austin, Atlanta and the rest. Search or click a city to follow it.

The strip under the map shows the followed city month by month: gray bars are hours of rain, each blue dot is one rain crash. In San Francisco the dots pile up under the wet winters (r = 0.56), while all crashes together ignore the rain entirely (r = minus 0.13). So the gap in slide 2 is not an averaging artifact, and the fleets kept operating through the rainy months.

5 of 5 · Severity

And the rain crashes that do happen are no worse

If rain crashes were rarer but more serious, the hypothesis could survive in another form. It does not. Ten percent of clear weather crashes injured someone, 8.4 percent of cloudy ones and 7.0 percent of rain crashes; on dry and wet roads the rate is identical at 9.6 percent. Nothing points up in rain.

What rain does change, in the narrative fields, is who the vehicle hits: a few more fixed objects and passenger cars, fewer SUVs and trucks. With 114 rain crashes that is a hint for the final project, not a finding for today.

So what

Hypothesis rejected. Rain is where the fleets are not, and the final visualization should say so

Finding

  • Rain is 3.9 percent of ADS crashes (114 of 2,945).
  • Against how often it rains, rain crashes run at 0.38 to 0.63 of expectation in all six metros; four metros exclude 1 on their own, and no rain neutral simulation comes near the real count.
  • Rain crashes are no more severe, and the pattern holds month by month.
  • Most likely cause: exposure. Fleets drive fewer miles in rain, by demand and by operator policy. The reports contain no miles, so "safer in rain" and "mostly absent in rain" cannot be separated yet.

Design decisions for the final

  • Weather becomes a filter and seasonal context, not a headline risk number.
  • The dot stays the unit: every view can dissolve back into the 2,945 reports, so a reader never loses what a percentage is made of.
  • Every rate gets a denominator and an interval; the thousand worlds are how the interval is explained.
  • D3 throughout: the canvas dot field, the simulation and the small multiples are the components, already sharing one metro selection; the final adds operator and time filters.
  • Next data: Waymo publishes rider only miles by city and month. Dividing rain crashes by rain miles turns the prediction into a true rate and retires the slider.

Testing plan

  • Five think aloud sessions with non experts: two MIDS classmates outside the team, three people who have ridden a robotaxi.
  • Tasks: "Which city has the most empty rings, and what does an empty ring mean?", "Is a rain crash more likely to injure someone?", "What is the blue line in the thousand worlds?" Success = correct answer under 20 seconds without help.
  • Measure task success, time, and where people hesitate (the rings and the slider are the suspected trouble spots).
  • After round one, rewrite the two worst labels and retest with two new people.
